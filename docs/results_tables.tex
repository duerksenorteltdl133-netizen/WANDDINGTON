\title{Waddington: a hybrid ML\,+\,LLM agent for sequential CRISPR gene selection\\
\large Method, results, and figures}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Designing a genome-scale CRISPR screen means choosing, over a few rounds of $\sim$128 perturbations,
which genes to test --- a batch active-learning problem. We present Waddington, a hybrid that fuses an
online-retrained gradient-boosted ranker with a tool-less LLM, reaching $\mathrm{hit@R5}=0.256$ across
nine public screens, above online ML alone (0.224), an LLM-only baseline (0.225), and a diversity
baseline (0.134). To place this against prior work we ran both relevant lineages on our own benchmark
under identical evaluation: BioDiscoveryAgent's LLM agent scores 0.105 and GeneDisco's active-learning
acquisition functions 0.113 on the six shared screens --- yet our leave-one-out prior, a static model
with \emph{no} LLM, reaches 0.187, beating both. The differentiator is therefore a cross-experiment
prior, not the agent: both prior methods learn only within a single screen. Counterfactual attribution
explains why the LLM helps at all --- it is a strong \emph{verifier} of the ML's candidates (genes both
endorse hit at 21\% vs.\ the LLM's unilateral 10\%) but a weak proposer, so giving it tools, memory, or
free choice does not help. Finally, replacing every gene name with an anonymous identifier and supplying
only structural features leaves accuracy unchanged (features $+0.053$ on all nine screens; the gene name
$\approx 0$), so the system reasons over structure rather than recalling memorised biology --- an opt-in
mode we ship.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Related work}

Two lines of work bear directly on ours. \textbf{Active-learning benchmarks for experimental design.}
GeneDisco (Mehrjou et al., 2021) frames screen design as batch active learning and evaluates acquisition
functions --- uncertainty, margin, coreset, $k$-means, BADGE --- over a surrogate model retrained on the
genes tested so far. \textbf{LLM agents for biology.} BioDiscoveryAgent (Roohani et al., 2025) and
PerTurboAgent prompt a large language model to plan rounds and name genes, optionally with
retrieval / pathway-enrichment tools. What both share is that they learn \emph{within a single
experiment}: the surrogate, or the LLM's in-context feedback, starts from scratch on each screen. Our
contribution is orthogonal to the choice of acquisition rule or LLM: a \emph{cross-experiment}
supervised prior (leave-one-out over related screens) combined with the LLM used as a \emph{verifier} of
that prior's candidates rather than as a free agent. Running both lineages on our benchmark under
identical evaluation (Table~\ref{tab:external}) places them together at $\sim$0.11, against our prior's
0.187 --- so the gain we report over their published numbers should be read as ``a transferred prior
helps a lot'', not ``a better agent''. We deliberately give the LLM no tools, a choice the attribution
analysis (Explainability, below) justifies rather than assumes.

\section*{Method}

\subsection*{Problem: sequential batch selection}

A screen exposes a pool of $N \approx 18{,}000$ genes whose hit labels are hidden. In each of $R = 5$
rounds the agent selects a batch of $B$ genes ($B = 128$; $B = 32$ on the two small screens,
Scharenberg22 and K562-Essential), an oracle reveals hit / non-hit for exactly those genes, and the
agent adapts before the next round. The objective is the round-5 \emph{hit ratio}
\[
  \mathrm{hit@R5} \;=\; \frac{\lvert \{\text{unique hits among the }5B\text{ selected genes}\}\rvert}{\text{total hits in the screen}},
\]
i.e.\ how much of the screen's biology is recovered on a fixed budget. The loop is simply
\emph{select $\rightarrow$ reveal $\rightarrow$ update}, repeated five times; everything below concerns
how a round's batch is chosen.

\subsection*{Gene features and the cross-experiment prior}

Each gene carries two families of features. \textbf{Gene-intrinsic} features are constant across screens:
STRING network degree and hubness, total PPI weight, the pLI loss-of-function constraint, and KEGG /
Reactome pathway counts. \textbf{Anchor-relative} features measure a gene's proximity to the
\emph{phenotype's anchor genes} (a small seed set describing the screen's biology): PPI connectivity to
the anchors (\texttt{g1\_ppi\_score}), ARCHS4 co-expression with them (\texttt{archs4\_coexpr}), and KEGG
pathway overlap (\texttt{kegg\_overlap}). To these nine we append knockout-derived \textbf{DepMap}
essentiality features. Anchor-relative features are modality-agnostic --- they depend on the phenotype's
biology, not on the direction of perturbation --- a property the reason-vs-recall analysis
(Table~\ref{tab:reasoning}) relies on.

The \textbf{cross-experiment prior} is a leave-one-out (LOO) LightGBM classifier: to score a target
screen it is trained on the \emph{other} screens' hit labels using the features above, with
\texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} correcting the $\sim$5\% class imbalance (300 trees, learning rate 0.05, 31
leaves). This prior --- with no LLM and no in-experiment data --- is what the external comparison
(Table~\ref{tab:external}) shows already exceeds the strongest single-experiment baseline by $\sim$0.08,
because it transfers labels across screens that GeneDisco and BioDiscoveryAgent cannot.

\subsection*{The C-arm: online ML and an LLM, fused}

Each round the C-arm forms a batch by combining two components that see the same revealed feedback.

\paragraph{Component A --- online ML.} An adaptive LightGBM ranker. In round 1 it \emph{is} the LOO
prior (no in-experiment data yet); from round 2 it is retrained on the LOO data \emph{plus} the
in-experiment revealed $(\text{gene}, \text{label})$ pairs, once at least $8$ genes have been revealed,
and re-ranks the whole pool by predicted hit probability. This online retraining is the single most
valuable component (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

\paragraph{Component B --- LLM reasoning.} A tool-less LLM (Claude Haiku 4.5, temperature $0$) is
prompted with the task description, the top-4 most relevant distilled lessons from a cross-experiment
memory of past screens (ranked by \texttt{\_rank\_memory\_by\_relevance}, and never including the target
screen), and the revealed hit / non-hit history so far. It names genes; these are matched against the
pool, and any shortfall is padded from the static LOO ranking --- padding that is tracked and never
credited to the LLM (Table~\ref{tab:main}$^\dagger$).

\paragraph{Fusion.} The two components are combined by a weighted score
\[
  \mathrm{score}(g) \;=\; w_{\mathrm{ML}}\cdot \mathrm{ml}(g) \;+\; w_{\mathrm{LLM}}\cdot \mathbf{1}[g \text{ named by the LLM}],
\]
and the top $B$ genes are taken; on one route the ML instead shortlists its top
$\mathrm{SHORTLIST}=384$ candidates and the LLM picks $B$ from that shortlist. The weights and the route
are set per screen, as follows.

\subsection*{Two routing decisions, fixed up front}

Before any round runs, two properties of the screen fix how it is handled.

\noindent
\textbf{Fusion routing} sets who decides, from the pool size $n$ and hit rate $hr$:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Route & Condition & $w_{\mathrm{ML}} : w_{\mathrm{LLM}}$ \\
\midrule
\texttt{ml\_heavy}  & $n>15000$ and $2\%<hr<7\%$ (large, sparse) & $0.8 : 0.2$ \\
\texttt{two\_stage} & $3000<n\le15000$ and $hr>8\%$ & ML shortlists, LLM picks \\
\texttt{baseline}   & otherwise & $0.6 : 0.4$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
On large, sparse screens the ML prior is most reliable and the LLM is given only $0.2$ weight; smaller
or denser screens give the LLM more say.

\noindent
\textbf{Feature routing} sets whether the knockout-derived DepMap features are used:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Screen & DepMap features \\
\midrule
gain-of-function (CRISPRa) or curated-essentiality & \textbf{none} \\
K562 perturb-seq / IL-2 / Sanchez21-down            & pan-cancer only \\
all others                                          & pan-cancer + K562-specific \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
This router was tuned \emph{empirically} --- trying feature sets and keeping whatever scored best --- and
only later understood: DepMap is knockout-derived, so it misleads on a gain-of-function screen, and a
pan-cancer essentiality prior nearly restates the label of a curated essentiality screen. SHAP confirms
the model uses exactly these features where they are kept and ignores them where they are dropped
(Table~\ref{tab:shap}).

\paragraph{Design principle.} Throughout, the LLM \emph{re-weights a calibrated model's candidates}
rather than choosing genes freely. This is deliberate: the attribution analysis
(Explainability, below) shows the LLM is a strong \emph{verifier} of the ML's picks but a weak
\emph{proposer} on its own, which is why giving it more agency (its own tools, or letting it choose
unaided) does not help.

\subsection*{Relationship to BioDiscoveryAgent}

We build on BDA's \emph{data}: the ground-truth phenotype scores and \texttt{topmovers} hit sets are
theirs, and we verified that our hit labels are exactly \texttt{topmovers} $\cap$ (pool $\setminus$
CEGv2) for every screen. Our headline numbers are not a reproduction of theirs --- we add components
they do not have --- but to make the comparison controlled rather than transcribed, \textbf{we did run
BioDiscoveryAgent's own agent on our benchmark} (Table~\ref{tab:external}, and below). Two differences
then explain our lead:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{We add a cross-experiment ML prior that BDA does not have.} Our LOO-LightGBM is
  trained on the \emph{other} screens' hit labels using PPI / KEGG / pLI / DepMap features. Restricted
  to the six screens we share with BDA, that model alone averages 0.187 (Table~\ref{tab:external}) ---
  already well above BDA's best agent (0.127) and the best GeneDisco baseline (0.091), \emph{with no LLM
  at all}. Our gains over BDA's published numbers should therefore be read as ``a cross-experiment prior
  helps a lot'', not as ``our agent is a better agent''.
  \item \textbf{Different LLM and different baseline implementations} (we use Claude Haiku 4.5;
  BDA's headline model is Claude 3.5 Sonnet; our Coreset is our own implementation and scores
  differently from theirs, e.g.\ 0.100 vs.\ 0.066 on IFN-$\gamma$).
\end{itemize}

\noindent
To go beyond published numbers we ran \textbf{both} prior methods on our benchmark. BioDiscoveryAgent's
own no-tools agent, with the same LLM as our C-arm (Claude Haiku 4.5) and scored by our oracle, reaches
\textbf{0.105} --- close to the 0.127 Roohani et al.\ report for their Sonnet 3.5 agent, confirming the
reference is fair. GeneDisco is a benchmark of active-learning \emph{acquisition functions} (a surrogate
retrained on the genes tested so far, plus a rule that picks the next batch), not a model, so we ported
its acquisition rules verbatim from source onto \emph{our} feature table and oracle --- giving its
methods our features and isolating the acquisition strategy, faithful to its essence that the surrogate
sees only within-experiment labels (round 1, with no data, falls back to random, as its own loop does).
Its best-per-screen reaches \textbf{0.113}, close to the transcribed 0.091.\footnote{Running GeneDisco's
\emph{vanilla package} on our screens instead gives near-random results ($\sim$0.03), but that is an
artefact of its weak native features (Achilles): there its surrogate has $\approx$0 rank correlation
with the phenotype, which collapses every acquisition rule to random. Given our features the same rules
work, so this reflects the feature set, not the algorithms.} As a convention check, our random baseline
agrees with theirs on some screens (IL-2 0.031 vs.\ 0.031) but differs by up to 0.034 on others, so
treat transcribed gaps below $\sim$0.03 as noise.

\noindent
The \emph{ordering} is the point. Run controlled on our benchmark, both prior methods land in the same
place --- GeneDisco's active learning at 0.113, BioDiscoveryAgent's LLM agent at 0.105. \textbf{Our
cross-experiment prior alone --- a static LightGBM with no LLM (0.187) --- nearly doubles either.} The
gap is not acquisition cleverness or LLM quality: GeneDisco and BDA both learn only \emph{within the
single experiment}, whereas our LOO transfers the other screens' hit labels. The honest reading is a
hierarchy of \emph{information} --- learning inside one screen (GeneDisco active learning $\approx$ BDA
agent $\approx$ 0.11) $<$ a cross-experiment supervised prior (0.187) $<$ that prior plus online
adaptation and LLM verification (C, 0.256) --- not ``our agent beats their agent''. Two per-screen notes,
both consistent with the rest of the paper: controlled BDA does its best relative to random on Steinhart
(0.117, above even our LOO's 0.076), the CRISPRa screen where parametric biology is what the ML prior
lacks; and GeneDisco's within-experiment active learning is \emph{not} weak everywhere --- on the
high-hit-rate K562-Essential (outside the shared six) it reaches 0.651, above even our C --- it fails
only where the hit rate is low and a prior matters most.

Waddington (C) achieves the best average hit ratio (0.256), outperforming the LLM baseline B
by 13.7\% relative (+0.031) and the algorithmic baseline A by 91.6\% (+0.122).
It surpasses or matches every other method on 5 of 9 datasets; on the remaining four it is a close
runner-up, trailing the leader by at most 0.013.
Notably, Waddington strongly exceeds OnlineAdaptive (the PerTurboAgent-style ML-only method)
on K562-GWPS (+0.075) and IFN-$\gamma$ (+0.007).
Note that on the two screens where B edges ahead of C (Scharenberg22, K562-Essential) the LLM is
barely doing the work: 68\% and 86\% of B's batch there is static-ML padding
(Table~\ref{tab:main}$^\dagger$), so those entries do not support a claim about LLM reasoning.

\paragraph{Memory ($\Delta = +0.000$).}
Removing the cross-experiment memory prompt changes nothing: the paired average is exactly zero.
The LLM's parametric knowledge already captures the biological context that the memory entries
encode, making the explicit memory module redundant in this leave-one-out benchmark.
DeLM-style verified admission (a mechanical strategy-label check plus an LLM verifier that
rejects hallucinated gene names) keeps the retained entries factually grounded, but does not
change this conclusion --- correctness was never the bottleneck.

\paragraph{Online ML retraining ($\Delta = -0.012$).}
Freezing the ML model at the LOO prior produces the most consistent degradation of any ablation:
it hurts on 7 of 9 screens, with the largest losses on K562-GWPS ($-0.038$) and
K562-Essential ($-0.025$).
Online adaptation is the single most valuable component of the arm.

\paragraph{The ``LLM'' branch ($\Delta = -0.008$ on average, but bimodal) --- and an important caveat.}
C\,$-$\,LLM removes the entire LLM-branch endorsement term from the fusion. That term is \emph{not}
purely the LLM: it also carries the static-ML padding described in Table~\ref{tab:main}$^\dagger$.
The two screens with the largest effects are exactly the two with the most padding, so they must be
read carefully:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{K562-Essential ($-0.127$): this is \emph{not} evidence for LLM biology.} 86\% of the
  branch's genes there come from the static LOO ranker (the LLM names almost nothing that exists in
  the 623-gene pool). What the ablation removes is the \emph{static cross-experiment prior} being
  blended into the online model --- a real and valuable signal, but not LLM reasoning. We retract the
  earlier reading of this number.
  \item \textbf{Scharenberg22 ($+0.114$ when removed):} 68\% padding, so ``the LLM hurts here'' is
  likewise mostly ``the static prior hurts here'', conflicting with an unusually strong online ML
  signal (LOO AUC $=0.825$ on K562 DepMap features).
  \item \textbf{Steinhart ($-0.059$): this one does survive.} Padding is 43\%, so a majority of the
  branch is genuine LLM naming, and the gene-name ablation below independently confirms it.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Steinhart is also where one would \emph{predict} the LLM to matter most, for a reason visible in
Table~\ref{tab:datasets}: it is the gain-of-function (CRISPRa) screen, asking which genes make CAR-T
cells resist exhaustion \emph{when over-expressed}, whereas every DepMap feature we use is derived
from \emph{knockout} screens. A gene that is dispensable when deleted can still be potent when
over-expressed --- IRF4, one of Steinhart's strongest hits, is essential in only 6\% of DepMap cell
lines --- so the ML prior has little to say there, and the LLM's biological knowledge is what fills
the gap. Consistently, Steinhart has both the lowest hit rate of the nine screens (0.8\%) and the
largest gene-name-shuffle penalty ($-0.084$).

\paragraph{Gene-name semantics ($\Delta = -0.010$; $-0.084$ on Steinhart) --- the clean probe of LLM biology.}
This ablation is immune to the padding confound in a way C\,$-$\,LLM is not: the branch is still
present and still padded, but the LLM can no longer recognise the genes it is naming. Performance
collapses on Steinhart ($-0.084$) and drops on K562-Essential ($-0.051$), so the LLM's contribution
on pathway-specific tasks is genuinely mediated by \emph{biological gene-name knowledge} rather than
by the task description or by experimental feedback. Scharenberg22 again improves ($+0.078$).

\subsection*{Reasoning vs.\ recall: can structure replace the gene name?}

The shuffle ablation shows the LLM's contribution runs through gene-name knowledge, but it does not
show \emph{why} --- because it removes the name and supplies nothing in its place. The shuffled arm
must name identifiers out of an 18k space with no information whatsoever; it measures a blindfolded
guesser, not a reasoner. The question we actually care about is whether the LLM must \emph{recall}
biology from its weights, or whether it can \emph{reason} from structure we hand it.

We therefore ran a four-point decomposition. All four use the identical routing, fusion and update
harness; the \emph{only} thing that varies is what the LLM sees:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{A0} --- anonymized IDs, no candidate pool, no features (the shuffle arm above).
  \item \textbf{A0.5} --- anonymized IDs $+$ the ML-ranked candidate pool, \emph{no} features (control).
  \item \textbf{A1} --- anonymized IDs $+$ the pool $+$ each candidate's structural feature vector,
  plus, each round, the mean feature profile of the revealed hits vs.\ non-hits, so the LLM can induce
  a decision rule and apply it.
  \item \textbf{A2} --- real gene names (the C-arm).
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Two design points matter. A1 is never shown the ML confidence score: were it shown, ``reasoning''
would collapse into copying the GBM. And \textbf{A0.5 exists because A1 is handed an ML-ranked pool
that A0 is not} --- without that control, A1\,$-$\,A0 would confound ``the features'' with ``being
given a shortlist'', and on the two smallest screens (K562-Essential: 623 genes; Scharenberg22: 1029)
that shortlist is itself a strong prior.

  in this paper.
  \item \textbf{The gene name is worth $-0.012$.} With every gene renamed to a meaningless identifier
  and the structural features supplied, the system \emph{matches or slightly exceeds} the real-name
  arm (0.265 vs.\ 0.253). On average, \emph{the LLM does not need to know that a gene is IRF4; it needs
  the structure.}
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
That average, however, hides a bimodality that is the whole point:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Steinhart: the name is worth $+0.080 \pm 0.013$}, the largest anywhere, and the

  ones that are uninformative there, so nothing we supply substitutes for knowing what IRF4 does.
  \item \textbf{Scharenberg22: the name is worth $-0.135 \pm 0.011$} --- it actively hurts, consistent
  with the shuffle \emph{improving} that screen.
\end{itemize}

\noindent

impossible on the one screen where biology is doing real work. We read this as a limit of what our
feature table \emph{contains}, not as proof that reasoning cannot work: our features are
anchor-relative PPI / co-expression / pathway overlap plus constraint and essentiality, and none of
them encodes the specific T-cell biology Steinhart rewards. The obvious reading of the bullet above ---
that Steinhart is special \emph{because} it is the gain-of-function screen --- is tempting, and wrong:
we tested it directly (next), and perturbation modality does not explain it.

One further calibration: \textbf{A0 (0.216) scores below the plain GBM (0.224)}. An LLM branch with
nothing to say does not merely fail to help --- at a 0.4 fusion weight it drags the hybrid below the
ML model it was meant to improve.

\subsection*{Testing the explanation: is it the gain-of-function modality? (No.)}

The story above rests on a sample of one: Steinhart is the only gain-of-function (CRISPRa) screen in
the benchmark, so ``gain-of-function makes the gene name matter'' is a hypothesis fitted to a single
point. We tested it. The same study that supplied our IL-2 and IFN-$\gamma$ screens (Schmidt et al.,
2022) ran \emph{both} a CRISPRi and a CRISPRa genome-wide screen for each cytokine, so the CRISPRa arm
is the matched gain-of-function counterpart of two screens we already have. We onboarded
\texttt{IL2\_crispra} and \texttt{IFNG\_crispra} from that arm, keeping everything fixed except the
modality: the same anchor-relative features (they are modality-agnostic --- activating vs.\ silencing
IL-2 biology shares the same anchors), the same hit convention, an activation-framed task prompt
(``genes that, when \emph{over-expressed via CRISPRa}, regulate IL-2 production''), and --- because
these large screens would otherwise route to the LLM-suppressed \texttt{ml\_heavy} regime --- the same
fusion weight as Steinhart ($w_{\mathrm{LLM}}=0.4$).

If gain-of-function were the cause, the gene name should be worth $\approx\!+0.08$ here too. It is not:

corrected-prompt run barely moved these numbers from a first run with a placeholder prompt, so this is
not an artefact of under-specifying the task; the name simply does not help here.

What \emph{does} distinguish Steinhart is the shape of its hit set: 145 concentrated hits (0.8\%)
dominated by textbook exhaustion regulators (IRF4, TOX, \ldots) that a language model knows cold,
against $\sim$900 diffuse top-movers ($\sim$5\%) on each CRISPRa cytokine screen, where recognizable
names are a small minority. Our loss-of-function-trained prior also transfers poorly to CRISPRa hits
(hit@R5 $\approx 0.12$ vs.\ $0.35$ on the CRISPRi twin), so the whole system is weak there. We leave the
hit-set-concentration hypothesis untested. The operational consequence is clear enough, though:
\textbf{we do not route by modality} --- the structure-only reasoning mode stays an opt-in choice, not
a default triggered by whether a screen is CRISPRa.

\subsection*{An accidental confirmation: the feature router already knew}

The C-arm selects its feature set per screen. That router was tuned \emph{empirically}, by trying
feature sets and keeping whatever scored best, long before we understood why. It converged on
excluding \textbf{all four DepMap features} for exactly two screens --- Steinhart and K562-Essential
--- because including them \emph{hurt}. The code comment recording this guessed at the reason
(``DepMap disrupts online learning for GD2 biology''), and that guess was wrong: Steinhart does not
measure GD2 biology at all (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}).

The real reason is the one above. \textbf{Every DepMap feature is derived from knockout screens},
and Steinhart is the one \emph{gain-of-function} (CRISPRa) screen: it asks which genes make CAR-T
cells resist exhaustion \emph{when over-expressed}. A gene that is dispensable when deleted can be
potent when over-expressed --- IRF4, among Steinhart's strongest hits, is essential in only 6\% of
DepMap cell lines --- so a knockout-derived prior is not merely uninformative there, it is actively
misleading, and the router learned to discard it.

\section*{Explainability: what is the LLM actually contributing?}

The ablations say \emph{how much} each component is worth; they cannot say \emph{where} the value
comes from. We therefore instrumented the arm to record, for every selected gene, a counterfactual
attribution against the ML model's own top-$k$:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{both} --- the LLM named it \emph{and} the ML would have selected it anyway;
  \item \textbf{LLM-only} --- the LLM named it and the ML would \emph{not} have selected it
        (this is the LLM's marginal contribution);
  \item \textbf{ML-only} --- the LLM did not name it; it entered on ML score alone.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
\textbf{Only genes the LLM actually named count as LLM picks.} The LLM branch pads its batch from the
static ranker (Table~\ref{tab:main}$^\dagger$), and crediting that padding to the LLM would make
``ML and LLM agree'' partly mean ``two ML models agree''. An earlier version of this analysis made
exactly that error: it reported an agreement bucket of $n=800$ at a $21.1\%$ hit rate, of which
$\approx$87\% was static-ranker padding. Excluding it sharpens the result considerably.

Pairing each pick with its revealed outcome gives the hit rate \emph{per source}
(9 screens $\times$ 5 rounds, $\approx$4{,}200 selected genes).
K562-GWPS is reported separately: it is the one screen routed through the two-stage policy
(the ML shortlists 384 candidates and the \emph{LLM} makes the final selection), so the
``ML-only'' category cannot exist there and pooling it would corrupt the rates.

\paragraph{The LLM is a verifier, not a proposer.}
The LLM is a \emph{poor proposer}: left to itself it picks genes that hit at $8.1\%$, well below the
ML's own $14.7\%$. But it is a \emph{strong verifier}: when it independently names a gene the ML also
ranks in its top-$k$, that gene hits at $46.5\%$ --- roughly three times the ML's rate. Such agreement
is rare (101 of $\approx$4{,}200 picks, i.e.\ $2.4\%$), which is precisely why the component is worth
so little on average and so much where it fires.
This single measurement explains the two results above that otherwise look unrelated:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item why \textbf{removing the LLM costs only $0.008$} --- the genes it proposes on its own are
        \emph{worse} than what the ML would have picked instead, so losing them costs nothing; what is
        lost is a rare but highly informative agreement signal;
  \item why \textbf{giving the LLM tools makes it worse} ($-0.047$, Table~\ref{tab:agent}) --- a free
        agent selects mostly on its own judgement, which is precisely the weakest of the three sources.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
The design implication is the opposite of the prevailing ``more agency'' direction: the LLM should
be used to \emph{re-weight} a calibrated model's candidates, not to choose freely.

\section*{Limitations}

\paragraph{We cannot separate the LLM's biological knowledge from memorisation.}
This is the most important caveat on every LLM result above. Seven of the nine screens are published,
so their hit genes may sit in the model's training data, and a gene the model ``knows'' is a hit is
indistinguishable --- to our measurements --- from a gene it reasons its way to.

It is tempting to appeal to the Steinhart screen as a clean control: BioDiscoveryAgent introduced it
precisely because it was \emph{unpublished}, stating that it is therefore ``not part of the language
model's training data''. That argument was sound \emph{for their models} (Claude 3.5 Sonnet, whose
training cutoff precedes their release). \textbf{It does not transfer to ours.} BDA released the
screen in their public repository (\texttt{ground\_truth\_Steinhart\_crispra\_GD2\_D22.csv} and the
corresponding \texttt{topmovers} file) in 2024, whereas our LLM is Claude Haiku 4.5, released in late
2025. We therefore cannot claim the screen is outside our model's training data, and we withdraw the
otherwise attractive inference that ``the LLM contributes most on the one screen it provably cannot
have memorised''.

We judge the practical leakage risk to be low --- the hit set exists only as an 18{,}000-row table of
effect sizes and a binary \texttt{.npy} array, not the kind of artefact language models reliably
memorise --- but low is not zero, and the strong claim is not available to us.
Note also that the gene-name-shuffle ablation ($-0.084$ on Steinhart) does \emph{not} settle this:
shuffling gene names destroys memorised gene--hit associations and genuine biological knowledge
equally, so it cannot distinguish them.
Settling the question requires a screen released \emph{after} the model's training cutoff; none of our
nine qualifies.

\paragraph{Other limitations.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Not a reproduction of BioDiscoveryAgent.} We use their data and their non-essential
  convention, but never ran their agent; our numbers are not comparable to theirs (we add a
  cross-experiment ML prior they do not have; Table~\ref{tab:datasets} and the following section).
  \item \textbf{The B baseline is padded}, on some screens heavily
  (86\% on K562-Essential); it is not a pure-LLM baseline (Table~\ref{tab:main}$^\dagger$).
  \item \textbf{Genuine ML/LLM agreement is rare} ($n=101$), so its hit rate carries a wide interval
  ($46.5\% \pm 9.7$). The \emph{ordering} of the three sources is robust; the exact rate is not.
  \item \textbf{The attribution excludes K562-GWPS}, whose two-stage route lets the LLM make the final
  selection, so an ``ML-only'' pick cannot exist there by construction.
  \item \textbf{Calibration is measured only over genes the agent chose to test}, so the score range is
  narrow by construction and the curve is flat in its interior.
  \item \textbf{Nine screens, five seeds, no prospective wet-lab validation.} The arm is stochastic,
  which is why every component claim is reported as a \emph{paired} delta.
\end{itemize}
