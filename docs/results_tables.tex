\subsection*{Relationship to BioDiscoveryAgent}

We use BDA's \emph{data}, not their method: the ground-truth phenotype scores and \texttt{topmovers}
hit sets are theirs, and we verified that our hit labels are exactly
\texttt{topmovers} $\cap$ (pool $\setminus$ CEGv2) for every screen.
\textbf{We did not run BioDiscoveryAgent, and the numbers in this paper are not a reproduction of
theirs and are not directly comparable to them.} Two differences matter:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{We add a cross-experiment ML prior that BDA does not have.} Our LOO-LightGBM is
  trained on the \emph{other} screens' hit labels using PPI / KEGG / pLI / DepMap features. That model
  alone averages 0.217 --- already above the best LLM hit ratio BDA reports on the screens we share.
  Our gains over BDA's published numbers should therefore be read as ``a cross-experiment prior helps
  a lot'', not as ``our agent is a better agent''.
  \item \textbf{Different LLM and different baseline implementations} (we use Claude Haiku 4.5;
  BDA's headline model is Claude 3.5 Sonnet; our Coreset is our own implementation and scores
  differently from theirs, e.g.\ 0.100 vs.\ 0.066 on IFN-$\gamma$).
\end{itemize}

\noindent
As a sanity check that the data and scoring are aligned, the \emph{random} baseline agrees closely
with BDA's reported random N/E values (IL-2: 0.031 vs.\ 0.031; IFN-$\gamma$: 0.029 vs.\ 0.035;
Sanchez21-dn: 0.029 vs.\ 0.034), while the method numbers diverge --- which is what one expects if the
benchmark is the same and the method is not.

\paragraph{Memory ($\Delta = +0.000$).}
Removing the cross-experiment memory prompt changes nothing: the paired average is exactly zero.
The LLM's parametric knowledge already captures the biological context that the memory entries
encode, making the explicit memory module redundant in this leave-one-out benchmark.
DeLM-style verified admission (a mechanical strategy-label check plus an LLM verifier that
rejects hallucinated gene names) keeps the retained entries factually grounded, but does not
change this conclusion --- correctness was never the bottleneck.

\paragraph{Online ML retraining ($\Delta = -0.012$).}
Freezing the ML model at the LOO prior produces the most consistent degradation of any ablation:
it hurts on 7 of 9 screens, with the largest losses on K562-GWPS ($-0.038$) and
K562-Essential ($-0.025$).
Online adaptation is the single most valuable component of the arm.

\paragraph{The ``LLM'' branch ($\Delta = -0.008$ on average, but bimodal) --- and an important caveat.}
C\,$-$\,LLM removes the entire LLM-branch endorsement term from the fusion. That term is \emph{not}
purely the LLM: it also carries the static-ML padding described in Table~\ref{tab:main}$^\dagger$.
The two screens with the largest effects are exactly the two with the most padding, so they must be
read carefully:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{K562-Essential ($-0.127$): this is \emph{not} evidence for LLM biology.} 86\% of the
  branch's genes there come from the static LOO ranker (the LLM names almost nothing that exists in
  the 623-gene pool). What the ablation removes is the \emph{static cross-experiment prior} being
  blended into the online model --- a real and valuable signal, but not LLM reasoning. We retract the
  earlier reading of this number.
  \item \textbf{Scharenberg22 ($+0.114$ when removed):} 68\% padding, so ``the LLM hurts here'' is
  likewise mostly ``the static prior hurts here'', conflicting with an unusually strong online ML
  signal (LOO AUC $=0.825$ on K562 DepMap features).
  \item \textbf{Steinhart ($-0.059$): this one does survive.} Padding is 43\%, so a majority of the
  branch is genuine LLM naming, and the gene-name ablation below independently confirms it.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Steinhart is also where one would \emph{predict} the LLM to matter most, for a reason visible in
Table~\ref{tab:datasets}: it is the gain-of-function (CRISPRa) screen, asking which genes make CAR-T
cells resist exhaustion \emph{when over-expressed}, whereas every DepMap feature we use is derived
from \emph{knockout} screens. A gene that is dispensable when deleted can still be potent when
over-expressed --- IRF4, one of Steinhart's strongest hits, is essential in only 6\% of DepMap cell
lines --- so the ML prior has little to say there, and the LLM's biological knowledge is what fills
the gap. Consistently, Steinhart has both the lowest hit rate of the nine screens (0.8\%) and the
largest gene-name-shuffle penalty ($-0.084$).

\paragraph{Gene-name semantics ($\Delta = -0.010$; $-0.084$ on Steinhart) --- the clean probe of LLM biology.}
This ablation is immune to the padding confound in a way C\,$-$\,LLM is not: the branch is still
present and still padded, but the LLM can no longer recognise the genes it is naming. Performance
collapses on Steinhart ($-0.084$) and drops on K562-Essential ($-0.051$), so the LLM's contribution
on pathway-specific tasks is genuinely mediated by \emph{biological gene-name knowledge} rather than
by the task description or by experimental feedback. Scharenberg22 again improves ($+0.078$).

\subsection*{Reasoning vs.\ recall: can structure replace the gene name?}

The shuffle ablation shows the LLM's contribution runs through gene-name knowledge, but it does not
show \emph{why} --- because it removes the name and supplies nothing in its place. The shuffled arm
must name identifiers out of an 18k space with no information whatsoever; it measures a blindfolded
guesser, not a reasoner. The question we actually care about is whether the LLM must \emph{recall}
biology from its weights, or whether it can \emph{reason} from structure we hand it.

We therefore ran a four-point decomposition. All four use the identical routing, fusion and update
harness; the \emph{only} thing that varies is what the LLM sees:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{A0} --- anonymized IDs, no candidate pool, no features (the shuffle arm above).
  \item \textbf{A0.5} --- anonymized IDs $+$ the ML-ranked candidate pool, \emph{no} features (control).
  \item \textbf{A1} --- anonymized IDs $+$ the pool $+$ each candidate's structural feature vector,
  plus, each round, the mean feature profile of the revealed hits vs.\ non-hits, so the LLM can induce
  a decision rule and apply it.
  \item \textbf{A2} --- real gene names (the C-arm).
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Two design points matter. A1 is never shown the ML confidence score: were it shown, ``reasoning''
would collapse into copying the GBM. And \textbf{A0.5 exists because A1 is handed an ML-ranked pool
that A0 is not} --- without that control, A1\,$-$\,A0 would confound ``the features'' with ``being
given a shortlist'', and on the two smallest screens (K562-Essential: 623 genes; Scharenberg22: 1029)
that shortlist is itself a strong prior.

\noindent
Three results, in order of how much they surprised us:

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{The shortlist is worth nothing} ($-0.005$, negative on 5 of 9). A1's gain is not an
  artefact of being handed ML-curated candidates.
  \item \textbf{The features are worth $+0.053$, and positive on all nine screens} ($+0.001$ to
  $+0.279$), every per-screen effect exceeding its seed s.d. --- the most consistent effect anywhere
  in this paper.
  \item \textbf{The gene name is worth $-0.012$.} With every gene renamed to a meaningless identifier
  and the structural features supplied, the system \emph{matches or slightly exceeds} the real-name
  arm (0.265 vs.\ 0.253). On average, \emph{the LLM does not need to know that a gene is IRF4; it needs
  the structure.}
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
That average, however, hides a bimodality that is the whole point:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Steinhart: the name is worth $+0.080 \pm 0.013$}, the largest anywhere, and the
  features recover almost nothing ($+0.017 \pm 0.008$; A1 at 0.065 is even \emph{below} A0 at 0.075,
  and the two error bars do not come close to overlapping). This is exactly the screen of
  Table~\ref{tab:shap}: the one gain-of-function (CRISPRa) screen, where every DepMap feature is
  knockout-derived and does not transfer. The features we are able to hand the LLM are precisely the
  ones that are uninformative there, so nothing we supply substitutes for knowing what IRF4 does.
  \item \textbf{Scharenberg22: the name is worth $-0.135 \pm 0.011$} --- it actively hurts, consistent
  with the shuffle \emph{improving} that screen.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
So ``reason, don't recall'' is achievable and essentially free on eight of nine screens, and
impossible on the one screen where biology is doing real work. We read this as a limit of what our
feature table \emph{contains}, not as proof that reasoning cannot work: our features are
anchor-relative PPI / co-expression / pathway overlap plus constraint and essentiality, and none of
them encodes gain-of-function T-cell biology. A richer feature table might close the Steinhart gap; a
knockout-derived one provably cannot.

One further calibration: \textbf{A0 (0.216) scores below the plain GBM (0.224)}. An LLM branch with
nothing to say does not merely fail to help --- at a 0.4 fusion weight it drags the hybrid below the
ML model it was meant to improve.

\subsection*{An accidental confirmation: the feature router already knew}

The C-arm selects its feature set per screen. That router was tuned \emph{empirically}, by trying
feature sets and keeping whatever scored best, long before we understood why. It converged on
excluding \textbf{all four DepMap features} for exactly two screens --- Steinhart and K562-Essential
--- because including them \emph{hurt}. The code comment recording this guessed at the reason
(``DepMap disrupts online learning for GD2 biology''), and that guess was wrong: Steinhart does not
measure GD2 biology at all (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}).

The real reason is the one above. \textbf{Every DepMap feature is derived from knockout screens},
and Steinhart is the one \emph{gain-of-function} (CRISPRa) screen: it asks which genes make CAR-T
cells resist exhaustion \emph{when over-expressed}. A gene that is dispensable when deleted can be
potent when over-expressed --- IRF4, among Steinhart's strongest hits, is essential in only 6\% of
DepMap cell lines --- so a knockout-derived prior is not merely uninformative there, it is actively
misleading, and the router learned to discard it.

Reading the SHAP attributions back out confirms that this is what the model actually does:

\section*{Explainability: what is the LLM actually contributing?}

The ablations say \emph{how much} each component is worth; they cannot say \emph{where} the value
comes from. We therefore instrumented the arm to record, for every selected gene, a counterfactual
attribution against the ML model's own top-$k$:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{both} --- the LLM named it \emph{and} the ML would have selected it anyway;
  \item \textbf{LLM-only} --- the LLM named it and the ML would \emph{not} have selected it
        (this is the LLM's marginal contribution);
  \item \textbf{ML-only} --- the LLM did not name it; it entered on ML score alone.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
\textbf{Only genes the LLM actually named count as LLM picks.} The LLM branch pads its batch from the
static ranker (Table~\ref{tab:main}$^\dagger$), and crediting that padding to the LLM would make
``ML and LLM agree'' partly mean ``two ML models agree''. An earlier version of this analysis made
exactly that error: it reported an agreement bucket of $n=800$ at a $21.1\%$ hit rate, of which
$\approx$87\% was static-ranker padding. Excluding it sharpens the result considerably.

Pairing each pick with its revealed outcome gives the hit rate \emph{per source}
(9 screens $\times$ 5 rounds, $\approx$4{,}200 selected genes).
K562-GWPS is reported separately: it is the one screen routed through the two-stage policy
(the ML shortlists 384 candidates and the \emph{LLM} makes the final selection), so the
``ML-only'' category cannot exist there and pooling it would corrupt the rates.

\paragraph{The LLM is a verifier, not a proposer.}
The LLM is a \emph{poor proposer}: left to itself it picks genes that hit at $8.1\%$, well below the
ML's own $14.7\%$. But it is a \emph{strong verifier}: when it independently names a gene the ML also
ranks in its top-$k$, that gene hits at $46.5\%$ --- roughly three times the ML's rate. Such agreement
is rare (101 of $\approx$4{,}200 picks, i.e.\ $2.4\%$), which is precisely why the component is worth
so little on average and so much where it fires.
This single measurement explains the two results above that otherwise look unrelated:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item why \textbf{removing the LLM costs only $0.008$} --- the genes it proposes on its own are
        \emph{worse} than what the ML would have picked instead, so losing them costs nothing; what is
        lost is a rare but highly informative agreement signal;
  \item why \textbf{giving the LLM tools makes it worse} ($-0.047$, Table~\ref{tab:agent}) --- a free
        agent selects mostly on its own judgement, which is precisely the weakest of the three sources.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
The design implication is the opposite of the prevailing ``more agency'' direction: the LLM should
be used to \emph{re-weight} a calibrated model's candidates, not to choose freely.

\section*{Limitations}

\paragraph{We cannot separate the LLM's biological knowledge from memorisation.}
This is the most important caveat on every LLM result above. Seven of the nine screens are published,
so their hit genes may sit in the model's training data, and a gene the model ``knows'' is a hit is
indistinguishable --- to our measurements --- from a gene it reasons its way to.

It is tempting to appeal to the Steinhart screen as a clean control: BioDiscoveryAgent introduced it
precisely because it was \emph{unpublished}, stating that it is therefore ``not part of the language
model's training data''. That argument was sound \emph{for their models} (Claude 3.5 Sonnet, whose
training cutoff precedes their release). \textbf{It does not transfer to ours.} BDA released the
screen in their public repository (\texttt{ground\_truth\_Steinhart\_crispra\_GD2\_D22.csv} and the
corresponding \texttt{topmovers} file) in 2024, whereas our LLM is Claude Haiku 4.5, released in late
2025. We therefore cannot claim the screen is outside our model's training data, and we withdraw the
otherwise attractive inference that ``the LLM contributes most on the one screen it provably cannot
have memorised''.

We judge the practical leakage risk to be low --- the hit set exists only as an 18{,}000-row table of
effect sizes and a binary \texttt{.npy} array, not the kind of artefact language models reliably
memorise --- but low is not zero, and the strong claim is not available to us.
Note also that the gene-name-shuffle ablation ($-0.084$ on Steinhart) does \emph{not} settle this:
shuffling gene names destroys memorised gene--hit associations and genuine biological knowledge
equally, so it cannot distinguish them.
Settling the question requires a screen released \emph{after} the model's training cutoff; none of our
nine qualifies.

\paragraph{Other limitations.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Not a reproduction of BioDiscoveryAgent.} We use their data and their non-essential
  convention, but never ran their agent; our numbers are not comparable to theirs (we add a
  cross-experiment ML prior they do not have; Table~\ref{tab:datasets} and the following section).
  \item \textbf{The B baseline is padded}, on some screens heavily
  (86\% on K562-Essential); it is not a pure-LLM baseline (Table~\ref{tab:main}$^\dagger$).
  \item \textbf{Genuine ML/LLM agreement is rare} ($n=101$), so its hit rate carries a wide interval
  ($46.5\% \pm 9.7$). The \emph{ordering} of the three sources is robust; the exact rate is not.
  \item \textbf{The attribution excludes K562-GWPS}, whose two-stage route lets the LLM make the final
  selection, so an ``ML-only'' pick cannot exist there by construction.
  \item \textbf{Calibration is measured only over genes the agent chose to test}, so the score range is
  narrow by construction and the curve is flat in its interior.
  \item \textbf{Nine screens, five seeds, no prospective wet-lab validation.} The arm is stochastic,
  which is why every component claim is reported as a \emph{paired} delta.
\end{itemize}
