\paragraph{Memory ($\Delta = +0.000$).}
Removing the cross-experiment memory prompt changes nothing: the paired average is exactly zero.
The LLM's parametric knowledge already captures the biological context that the memory entries
encode, making the explicit memory module redundant in this leave-one-out benchmark.
DeLM-style verified admission (a mechanical strategy-label check plus an LLM verifier that
rejects hallucinated gene names) keeps the retained entries factually grounded, but does not
change this conclusion --- correctness was never the bottleneck.

\paragraph{Online ML retraining ($\Delta = -0.012$).}
Freezing the ML model at the LOO prior produces the most consistent degradation of any ablation:
it hurts on 7 of 9 screens, with the largest losses on K562-GWPS ($-0.038$) and
K562-Essential ($-0.025$).
Online adaptation is the single most valuable component of the arm.

\paragraph{LLM ($\Delta = -0.008$ on average, but bimodal).}
The average understates the LLM: it is decisive where molecular pathway knowledge cannot be
recovered from PPI or DepMap features --- K562-Essential ($-0.127$) and Steinhart ($-0.059$) ---
and actively harmful on Scharenberg22 ($+0.114$ when removed), where the LLM's biological prior
conflicts with an unusually strong ML signal (LOO AUC $=0.825$ on K562 DepMap features).
The near-zero mean is therefore a cancellation of large opposite effects, not a small effect.

\paragraph{Gene-name semantics ($\Delta = -0.010$; $-0.084$ on Steinhart).}
Replacing gene names with anonymous identifiers collapses performance on pathway-specific tasks
(Steinhart $-0.084$, K562-Essential $-0.051$), confirming that the LLM's contribution on those tasks
is mediated by \emph{biological gene-name knowledge} rather than by the task description or by
experimental feedback. Scharenberg22 again improves ($+0.078$), mirroring the C\,$-$\,LLM finding.

\section*{Does giving the LLM tools help? (No.)}

The natural next step from a pipeline is a \emph{tool-using agent} in the BioDiscoveryAgent /
PerTurboAgent mould: let the LLM plan, call an ML-ranking tool and a pathway-enrichment tool, and
commit a batch itself. We implemented exactly that (a task-specialised harness with a JSON action
protocol; \texttt{ml\_rank} / \texttt{enrich} / \texttt{finish}; no agent framework) and benchmarked
it against the pipeline on identical screens and budgets.

The agent wins on exactly one screen (Scharenberg22, $+0.116$), where its runtime pathway-enrichment
tool pays off, and loses most on the two genome-wide screens where the ML prior is strongest.
The failure mode is instructive: given the freedom to act, the LLM overrides a model that is better
calibrated than it is. \textbf{Constraining the LLM to a fixed fusion policy is not a limitation of
the pipeline --- it is the reason the pipeline wins.}

\subsection*{Transplanting the agent's one good idea}

Since enrichment was the agent's only advantage, we transplanted it into the pipeline (Enrichr over
the hits revealed so far, injected into the LLM prompt each round, gated to significant and coherent
pathways), and separately tested an externalised \emph{skill library} distilled from past experiments.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{%
  Agent-style augmentations of the pipeline, as \textbf{paired} deltas over the same runs.
  Both are within noise of zero.
}
\label{tab:aug}
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{8pt}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Augmentation & C & C\,$+$\,augmentation & $\Delta$ (paired) \\
\midrule
Runtime pathway enrichment (gated) & 0.260 & 0.263 & $+0.003$ \\
Externalised skill library         & 0.258 & 0.259 & $+0.001$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Runtime enrichment captures the Scharenberg22 gain ($+0.061$ on that screen) without the collateral
damage the free agent suffered, but nets only $+0.003$ overall: on genome-wide essentiality screens
the enriched terms are highly significant yet \emph{non-selective} (ribosome, proteasome,
spliceosome), so ``prioritise genes in these pathways'' barely narrows a 9{,}000-gene pool.
The skill library adds $+0.001$. Together with the memory ablation ($+0.000$), this is a consistent
negative result about \emph{externalisation}: memory, skills and retrieved pathways all add
$\approx 0$ once the model already has its parametric knowledge plus the hits it has observed.

\section*{Explainability: what is the LLM actually contributing?}

The ablations say \emph{how much} each component is worth; they cannot say \emph{where} the value
comes from. We therefore instrumented the arm to record, for every selected gene, a counterfactual
attribution against the ML model's own top-$k$:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{both} --- the LLM named it \emph{and} the ML would have selected it anyway;
  \item \textbf{LLM-only} --- the LLM named it and the ML would \emph{not} have selected it
        (this is the LLM's marginal contribution);
  \item \textbf{ML-only} --- the LLM did not name it; it entered on ML score alone.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Pairing each pick with its revealed outcome gives the hit rate \emph{per source}
(9 screens $\times$ 5 rounds, $\approx$4{,}200 selected genes).
K562-GWPS is reported separately: it is the one screen routed through the two-stage policy
(the ML shortlists 384 candidates and the \emph{LLM} makes the final selection), so the
``ML-only'' category cannot exist there and pooling it would corrupt the rates.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{%
  Hit rate by attribution source, over the eight weighted-fusion screens.
  \emph{Pooled} counts every selected gene once; \emph{macro} averages the per-screen rates
  (screens with $\ge 10$ picks in that category).
  Both aggregations agree on the headline: agreement between the two components is by far the
  strongest signal, and the LLM's unilateral picks are \emph{not} better than the ML's.
}
\label{tab:attrib}
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{8pt}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Source & Genes selected & Hit rate (pooled) & Hit rate (macro) \\
\midrule
\textbf{ML $+$ LLM agree} & 800  & \best{21.1\%} & \best{22.5\%} \\
ML only                   & 1{,}851 & 15.3\% & 13.4\% \\
LLM only                  & 1{,}509 & 10.2\% & 13.2\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{06_attribution.png}
\caption{Left: what each round's batch is made of, pooled over the eight weighted-fusion screens.
Right: the hit rate of each source. Genes that \emph{both} components endorse hit at roughly twice

\paragraph{The LLM is a verifier, not a proposer.}
Genes that both components endorse hit at $21.1\%$ --- about $1.4\times$ the ML's own rate and
$2\times$ the rate of the genes the LLM introduces unilaterally.
The LLM's \emph{marginal} picks are no better than the ML's (worse when pooled, equal when
macro-averaged, so the honest reading is ``no better'').
This single measurement explains the two results above that otherwise look unrelated:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item why \textbf{removing the LLM costs only $0.008$} --- the genes it adds on its own are not
        better than what the ML would have picked instead; its value is concentrated in the
        \emph{agreement} signal it provides over the ML's candidates;
  \item why \textbf{giving the LLM tools makes it worse} ($-0.047$, Table~\ref{tab:agent}) --- the
        free agent selects mostly on its own judgement, which is precisely the weakest source.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
The design implication is the opposite of the prevailing ``more agency'' direction: the LLM should
be used to \emph{re-weight} a calibrated model's candidates, not to choose freely.
