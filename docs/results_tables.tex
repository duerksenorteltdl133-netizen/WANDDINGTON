\title{Waddington: A Constrained Conversational ML\,--\,LLM Agent for Sequential CRISPR Gene Selection%
\thanks{We keep the term \emph{agent} deliberately: Waddington runs a stateful sequential loop, observes
experimental feedback each round, updates its actions across rounds, and is driven through a
natural-language conversational interface with the researcher. \emph{Constrained conversational}
signals what it is \emph{not} --- a freely planning tool-using agent. Its policy deliberately confines the
LLM to \emph{verifying and re-ranking} candidates proposed by a calibrated ML model rather than choosing
genes on its own; the paper's central finding (\S``a freely planning tool-using agent underperforms'')
is that this constraint is what makes it work.}\\
\large Method, results, and figures}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Designing a genome-scale CRISPR screen means choosing, over a few rounds of $\sim$128 perturbations,
which genes to test --- a batch active-learning problem. We present Waddington, a hybrid that fuses an
online-retrained gradient-boosted ranker with a tool-less LLM, reaching $\mathrm{hit@R5}=0.251$ across
nine public screens under a configuration fixed \emph{without target-screen outcomes} --- a global fusion
weight selected on non-target screens, a metadata-only target feature policy, and no privileged anchor
features --- above online ML alone (0.224), a padded LLM baseline (0.225), and a diversity baseline (0.134).
(An earlier target-aware routed variant scores 0.256, with no detected difference; we report the
leakage-free system.) To place this against prior work we ran both relevant lineages on our own benchmark
under identical evaluation: BioDiscoveryAgent's LLM agent scores 0.105 and GeneDisco's active-learning
acquisition functions 0.113 on the six shared screens --- yet our leave-one-out prior, a static model
with \emph{no} LLM, reaches 0.187, beating both. The differentiator is therefore a cross-experiment
prior, not the agent: both prior methods learn only within a single screen. A simple cross-screen
hit-frequency baseline matches the learned prior, so most of this transferred signal is recurrent hits
and generic essentiality, not novel phenotype-specific biology. Counterfactual attribution explains why
the LLM helps at all --- it is a strong \emph{verifier} but a weak proposer: after adjusting for the ML
score, endorsement still predicts hits (adjusted odds ratio $1.68$, $95\%$ CI $[1.05, 2.52]$),
concentrated in the top ML-score tertile where endorsed genes hit at $43.1\%$ vs.\ $17.4\%$; so, in our
tested setup (one action set, prompt, and budget), adding tools, explicit memory, or unconstrained action
selection does not help. Finally, replacing every gene name with an anonymous identifier and supplying
structural features instead yields \emph{similar mean performance} to the real-name condition, with no
detected difference --- and this holds after the anchor-relative features later identified as privileged
are removed (structure vs.\ names, paired $-0.007$, $95\%$ CI $[\!-\!0.041, 0.029]$, which does not
establish formal equivalence); a trivial linear rule on those features does about as well as the LLM. The
results are thus \emph{consistent with structure-sufficiency}, not LLM reasoning per se, though individual
tasks such as Steinhart still benefit from gene-name semantics and we cannot separate biological knowledge
from memorisation. We ship the anonymized mode as an opt-in.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Related work}

Two lines of work bear directly on ours. \textbf{Active-learning benchmarks for experimental design.}
GeneDisco (Mehrjou et al., 2021) frames screen design as batch active learning and evaluates acquisition
functions --- uncertainty, margin, coreset, $k$-means, BADGE --- over a surrogate model retrained on the
genes tested so far. \textbf{LLM agents for biology.} BioDiscoveryAgent (Roohani et al., 2025) and
PerTurboAgent prompt a large language model to plan rounds and name genes, optionally with
retrieval / pathway-enrichment tools. What both share is that they learn \emph{within a single
experiment}: the surrogate, or the LLM's in-context feedback, starts from scratch on each screen. Our
contribution is orthogonal to the choice of acquisition rule or LLM: a \emph{cross-experiment}
supervised prior (leave-one-out over related screens) combined with the LLM used as a \emph{verifier} of
that prior's candidates rather than as a free agent. Running both lineages on our benchmark under
identical evaluation (Table~\ref{tab:external}) places them together at $\sim$0.11, against our prior's
0.187 --- so the gain we report over their published numbers should be read as ``a transferred prior
helps a lot'', not ``a better agent''. We deliberately give the LLM no tools, a choice the attribution
analysis (Explainability, below) justifies rather than assumes.

\section*{Method}

\subsection*{Problem: sequential batch selection}

A screen exposes a pool of $N \approx 18{,}000$ genes whose hit labels are hidden. In each of $R = 5$
rounds the agent selects a batch of $B$ genes ($B = 128$; $B = 32$ on the two small screens,
Scharenberg22 and K562-Essential), an oracle reveals hit / non-hit for exactly those genes, and the
agent adapts before the next round. The objective is the round-5 \emph{hit ratio}
\[
  \mathrm{hit@R5} \;=\; \frac{\lvert \{\text{unique hits among the }5B\text{ selected genes}\}\rvert}{\text{total hits in the screen}},
\]
i.e.\ how much of the screen's biology is recovered on a fixed budget. The loop is simply
\emph{select $\rightarrow$ reveal $\rightarrow$ update}, repeated five times; everything below concerns
how a round's batch is chosen.

\subsection*{Gene features and the cross-experiment prior}

Each gene carries two families of features. \textbf{Gene-intrinsic} features are constant across screens:
STRING network degree and hubness, total PPI weight, the pLI loss-of-function constraint, and KEGG /
Reactome pathway counts. \textbf{Anchor-relative} features measure a gene's proximity to the
\emph{phenotype's anchor genes} (a small seed set describing the screen's biology): PPI connectivity to
the anchors (\texttt{g1\_ppi\_score}), ARCHS4 co-expression with them (\texttt{archs4\_coexpr}), and KEGG
pathway overlap (\texttt{kegg\_overlap}). To these nine we append knockout-derived \textbf{DepMap}
essentiality features. Anchor-relative features are modality-agnostic --- they depend on the phenotype's
biology, not on the direction of perturbation --- a property the reason-vs-recall analysis
(Table~\ref{tab:reasoning}) relies on.

The \textbf{cross-experiment prior} is a leave-one-out (LOO) LightGBM classifier: to score a target
screen it is trained on the \emph{other} screens' hit labels using the features above, with
\texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} correcting the $\sim$5\% class imbalance (300 trees, learning rate 0.05, 31
leaves). This prior --- with no LLM and no in-experiment data --- is what the external comparison
(Table~\ref{tab:external}) shows already exceeds the strongest single-experiment baseline by $\sim$0.08,
because it transfers labels across screens that GeneDisco and BioDiscoveryAgent cannot.

\subsection*{The C-arm: online ML and an LLM, fused}

Each round the C-arm forms a batch by combining two components that see the same revealed feedback.

\paragraph{Component A --- online ML.} An adaptive LightGBM ranker. In round 1 it \emph{is} the LOO
prior (no in-experiment data yet); from round 2 it is retrained on the LOO data \emph{plus} the
in-experiment revealed $(\text{gene}, \text{label})$ pairs, once at least $8$ genes have been revealed,
and re-ranks the whole pool by predicted hit probability. This online retraining is the single most
valuable component (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

\paragraph{Component B --- LLM endorsement (candidate verification).} A tool-less LLM (Claude Haiku 4.5,
temperature $0$) is
prompted with the task description, the top-4 most relevant distilled lessons from a cross-experiment
memory of past screens (ranked by \texttt{\_rank\_memory\_by\_relevance}, and never including the target
screen), and the revealed hit / non-hit history so far. It names genes; these are matched against the
pool, and any shortfall is padded from the static LOO ranking --- padding that is tracked and never
credited to the LLM (Table~\ref{tab:main}$^\dagger$).

\paragraph{Fusion.} The two components are combined by a weighted score
\[
  \mathrm{score}(g) \;=\; w_{\mathrm{ML}}\cdot \mathrm{ml}(g) \;+\; w_{\mathrm{LLM}}\cdot \mathbf{1}[g \text{ named by the LLM}],
\]
and the top $B$ genes are taken. In the reported system the weight is a \emph{single global constant},
not a per-screen choice.

\subsection*{The final configuration: fixed without target-screen outcomes}

The system we report fixes everything a screen needs \emph{before} it runs, and never uses the target's
realized labels. Two ingredients: a global fusion weight, selected once on the \emph{non-target} screens
(below), and a target feature policy that is a pure function of pre-experiment metadata --- pool size,
perturbation modality, cell line.

\noindent
\textbf{Fusion weight.} A single global $w_{\mathrm{LLM}}=0.2$ ($w_{\mathrm{ML}}=0.8$) on every screen.
That value is chosen once, by leave-one-out over the \emph{other} screens (it wins in every fold); no
per-screen routing and no hit rate are involved.

\noindent
\textbf{Feature policy}, a pure function of experimental-design metadata:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Metadata of the screen & DepMap features \\
\midrule
gain-of-function (CRISPRa) or curated-essentiality panel & \textbf{none} \\
cell line $=$ K562                                        & pan-cancer only (drop K562-matched) \\
otherwise                                                 & pan-cancer + K562-specific \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
DepMap is knockout-derived, so it misleads on a gain-of-function screen; a pan-cancer essentiality prior
nearly restates a curated-essentiality label; and a cell-line-matched essentiality feature is a proxy
label on its own cell line. SHAP confirms the model uses these features where kept and ignores them where
dropped (Table~\ref{tab:shap}). \textbf{The anchor-relative features are dropped entirely}: a leakage
audit found $51\%$ of anchors are themselves target hits (privileged information), and removing them costs
only $0.009$ (\S\ref{sec:honest-router}).

\noindent
\textbf{A legacy target-aware router (analyzed in \S\ref{sec:honest-router}, not used here).} An earlier
version set $w_{\mathrm{LLM}}$ per screen from the \emph{realized} hit rate
(\texttt{ml\_heavy}\,$0.8{:}0.2$ / \texttt{two\_stage} / \texttt{baseline}\,$0.6{:}0.4$) and kept the
anchor features. It scores $0.256$ (no detected difference from the final system) but reads a test-set
statistic, so we retain it only as a robustness comparison. The detailed ablations, attribution, and SHAP
were computed on this near-performing predecessor and are kept as supporting analyses; we did not
re-estimate the individual component effects under the final configuration, and do not assume they all
transfer quantitatively.

\paragraph{Why the reported system avoids the legacy router.} The legacy route reads the realized hit
rate --- a statistic of the test set, determining the route on seven of the nine screens --- and keeps the
privileged anchor features. That is precisely why the reported system uses neither. Fixing the weight
without target-screen outcomes (on the training screens) and the feature policy from pre-experiment
metadata, and dropping the anchor features, reaches $0.251$, with no detected difference from the routed
$0.256$ (paired $-0.005$, CI $[\!-\!0.018,0.007]$), and still beats the routing-free LOO prior by
$+0.034$ on 8 of 9 screens (\S\ref{sec:honest-router}). The main external claim never used the router
anyway: the cross-experiment prior that beats single-experiment active learning and LLM agents ($0.187$
vs.\ $\approx\!0.11$, Table~\ref{tab:external}) is the routing-free static LOO-LightGBM. We keep the legacy
variant only because the detailed ablations, attribution, and SHAP were computed on it (statistically
identical).

\paragraph{Design principle.} Throughout, the LLM \emph{re-weights a calibrated model's candidates}
rather than choosing genes freely. This is deliberate: the attribution analysis
(Explainability, below) shows the LLM is a strong \emph{verifier} of the ML's picks but a weak
\emph{proposer} on its own, which is why giving it more agency (its own tools, or letting it choose
unaided) does not help.

Pre-experiment: (w\_ml, w\_llm) from metadata (n\_pool, modality, cell line) \\

\> batch = top\_B(score, exclude=selected);\quad pad from static LOO if short\quad// pad not credited \\
\> revealed = oracle.reveal(batch);\quad selected $\cup$= batch;\quad online\_ranker.add(revealed) \\

\end{tabbing}
\end{minipage}}
\end{center}

\noindent
\textbf{A worked round (IFN-$\gamma$, round 1).} Route \texttt{ml\_heavy}
($w_{\mathrm{ML}}{=}0.8$, $w_{\mathrm{LLM}}{=}0.2$), $B{=}128$; the ML ranks all 17{,}785 genes and the LLM
independently names 118. The fused top-128 is $16$ \emph{both} (ML top-$k$ \emph{and} LLM-named), $97$
\emph{ML-only}, $15$ \emph{LLM-only}. Outcomes: the \emph{both} bucket hits $14/16$ ($88\%$; e.g.\ ZAP70,
VAV1, GRAP2, all ML${\approx}0.98$), ML-only $37/97$ ($38\%$; CD5, CD247, MAP4K1), LLM-only $6/15$ ($40\%$;
the LLM lifted mid-ranked genes such as TSC2 and RHOA, ML${\approx}0.82$, into the batch). The round finds
$57/128$ hits. In one round you can see the whole thesis: the batch is ML-dominated, and the LLM's mark
concentrates hits in the small bucket both endorse. (This single round is a mechanistic illustration, not
a representative rate: its $88\%$ agreement hit rate runs above the $46.5\%$ pooled agreement rate of
Table~\ref{tab:attrib}; the pattern, not the level, is the point.)

\subsection*{The conversational interface}

A scientist drives Waddington in natural language rather than by editing config. A lightweight intent
router (itself an LLM call) parses each free-text message into a structured action --- \texttt{suggest}
one batch, run an interactive \texttt{experiment} campaign, \texttt{simulate} end-to-end,
\texttt{register} a new phenotype, or \texttt{chat} --- and extracts the phenotype, batch size, number of
rounds, the run mode, and any genes the scientist reports as hits or non-hits in that message. An
\texttt{experiment} then runs the round loop conversationally: the system proposes a batch, the scientist
commits, outcomes are revealed --- either by the benchmark oracle or, in \emph{upload} mode, by the
scientist supplying their own screen readout (a MAGeCK or CSV file, from which hits are derived) --- and
the revealed hits and non-hits become cross-round state that conditions the next proposal. Biology outside
the nine benchmark screens is handled by \texttt{register} (phenotype name, task, measurement, seed
genes), after which the same pipeline ranks it.

Crucially, the conversational LLM is confined to \emph{intake and narration}: every gene choice is made
by the constrained selection policy above, never by the conversational chat model. So the interface adds
natural-language control (goal, budget, round-by-round feedback, new phenotypes) without relaxing the
ML-calibration constraint that the rest of this paper argues for. We describe this interface as a system
component and do not benchmark it; features such as candidate veto or rationale-on-demand are natural
extensions we have not yet built.

\subsection*{Relationship to BioDiscoveryAgent}

We build on BDA's \emph{data}: the ground-truth phenotype scores and \texttt{topmovers} hit sets are
theirs, and we verified that our hit labels are exactly \texttt{topmovers} $\cap$ (pool $\setminus$
CEGv2) for every screen. Our headline numbers are not a reproduction of theirs --- we add components
they do not have --- but to make the comparison controlled rather than transcribed, \textbf{we did run
BioDiscoveryAgent's own agent on our benchmark} (Table~\ref{tab:external}, and below). Two differences
then explain our lead:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{We add a cross-experiment ML prior that BDA does not have.} Our LOO-LightGBM is
  trained on the \emph{other} screens' hit labels using PPI / KEGG / pLI / DepMap features. Restricted
  to the six screens we share with BDA, that model alone averages 0.187 (Table~\ref{tab:external}) ---
  already well above BDA's best agent (0.127) and the best GeneDisco baseline (0.091), \emph{with no LLM
  at all}. Our gains over BDA's published numbers should therefore be read as ``a cross-experiment prior
  helps a lot'', not as ``our agent is a better agent''.
  \item \textbf{Different LLM and different baseline implementations} (we use Claude Haiku 4.5;
  BDA's headline model is Claude 3.5 Sonnet; our Coreset is our own implementation and scores
  differently from theirs, e.g.\ 0.100 vs.\ 0.066 on IFN-$\gamma$).
\end{itemize}

\noindent
To go beyond published numbers we ran \textbf{both} prior methods on our benchmark. BioDiscoveryAgent's
own no-tools agent, with the same LLM as our C-arm (Claude Haiku 4.5) and scored by our oracle, reaches
\textbf{0.105} --- close to the 0.127 Roohani et al.\ report for their Sonnet 3.5 agent, confirming the
reference is fair. GeneDisco is a benchmark of active-learning \emph{acquisition functions} (a surrogate
retrained on the genes tested so far, plus a rule that picks the next batch), not a model, so we ported
its acquisition rules verbatim from source onto \emph{our} feature table and oracle --- giving its
methods our features and isolating the acquisition strategy, faithful to its essence that the surrogate
sees only within-experiment labels (round 1, with no data, falls back to random, as its own loop does).
Its best-per-screen reaches \textbf{0.113}, close to the transcribed 0.091.\footnote{Running GeneDisco's
\emph{vanilla package} on our screens instead gives near-random results ($\sim$0.03), but that is an
artefact of its weak native features (Achilles): there its surrogate has $\approx$0 rank correlation
with the phenotype, which collapses every acquisition rule to random. Given our features the same rules
work, so this reflects the feature set, not the algorithms.} As a convention check, our random baseline
agrees with theirs on some screens (IL-2 0.031 vs.\ 0.031) but differs by up to 0.034 on others, so
treat transcribed gaps below $\sim$0.03 as noise.

\noindent
The \emph{ordering} is the point. Run controlled on our benchmark, both prior methods land in the same
place --- GeneDisco's active learning at 0.113, BioDiscoveryAgent's LLM agent at 0.105. \textbf{Our
cross-experiment prior alone --- a static LightGBM with no LLM (0.187) --- nearly doubles either.} The
gap is not acquisition cleverness or LLM quality: GeneDisco and BDA both learn only \emph{within the
single experiment}, whereas our LOO transfers the other screens' hit labels. The honest reading is a
hierarchy of \emph{information}, all on these same six screens --- learning inside one screen (GeneDisco
active learning $\approx$ BDA agent $\approx$ 0.11) $<$ a cross-experiment supervised prior (LOO 0.187)
$<$ that prior plus online adaptation and LLM verification (C, 0.217 here; 0.251 over all nine screens,
Table~\ref{tab:main}) --- not ``our agent beats their agent''. Two per-screen notes,
both consistent with the rest of the paper: controlled BDA does its best relative to random on Steinhart
(0.117, above even our LOO's 0.076), the CRISPRa screen where parametric biology is what the ML prior
lacks; and GeneDisco's within-experiment active learning is \emph{not} weak everywhere --- on the
high-hit-rate K562-Essential (outside the shared six) it reaches 0.651, above even our C --- it fails
only where the hit rate is low and a prior matters most.

\subsection*{What the cross-experiment prior actually learns (and what it does not)}

The prior is the largest single source of our advantage, so it deserves scrutiny: is its gain genuine
phenotype-specific transfer, or something more trivial --- sibling-screen leakage, generic essentiality,
plain hit recurrence? We probe this with the LOO LightGBM alone (no LLM), on a uniform all-feature
baseline so the feature-family ablation is clean; this scores 0.243 averaged over the nine screens
(slightly above the routed 0.187/0.217 used elsewhere, as it keeps DepMap on every screen). Three probes,
and the picture is sobering.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Sibling leakage is real.} Our nine screens contain three same-study pairs
  (IFN-$\gamma$/IL-2, Sanchez21/-down, K562-Essential/-GWPS). Re-training with the target's sibling
  \emph{also} removed drops the prior from 0.243 to 0.199 on average, and from 0.248 to \textbf{0.182}
  on the six paired screens --- concentrated on IL-2 ($-0.098$) and K562-Essential ($-0.222$), whose
  Perturb-seq sibling shares much of its hit structure. So ``generalises to a new screen'' partly means
  ``transfers from a sister screen in the same study''.
  \item \textbf{Most of the signal is generic essentiality.} Restricted to the DepMap features alone the
  prior scores 0.230 --- nearly the full 0.243 --- while the phenotype-specific features
  (intrinsic $+$ anchor, \emph{no} DepMap) give 0.219. The prior leans heavily on knockout essentiality,
  which is not screen-specific.
  \item \textbf{A trivial baseline matches it.} Ranking a gene simply by \emph{how often it is a hit in
  the other screens} --- no model, no features --- also averages \textbf{0.243}. The LightGBM adds
  nothing over counting cross-screen hit recurrence.
  \item \textbf{The anchor features leak, but are not load-bearing.} The three anchor-relative features
  (proximity to a phenotype's seed genes) are built from a hand-picked anchor set that, by our own audit,
  is \textbf{51\% composed of the screen's own hits} (42 of 82 anchors; e.g.\ 10/12 for IL-2, 9/10 for
  K562-GWPS) --- genuine privileged information. Yet dropping \emph{every} anchor-relative feature costs
  the prior only $0.015$ ($0.243\!\rightarrow\!0.228$): the anchor family is the weakest on its own
  ($0.156$), and the label-free DepMap ($0.230$) and intrinsic-topology ($0.218$) features are what carry
  it. The leak is real; its contribution to the headline is not.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
A novel-hit analysis explains the mechanism: the prior's gains sit on screens whose hits recur
(K562-Essential has only 6\% novel hits, IL-2 41\%), and it is near random on screens dominated by novel,
phenotype-specific hits (Carnevale22 80\% novel, Sanchez21 72\%). \textbf{The cross-experiment prior
succeeds by exploiting recurrence and essentiality, not by discovering new phenotype-specific biology.}

This qualifies but does not overturn the headline. Even after all three corrections --- siblings excluded
(0.182), DepMap removed (0.219), or anchor features removed (0.228) --- the prior stays well above the
single-experiment methods ($\sim$0.11, Table~\ref{tab:external}). The honest claim is therefore narrower and, we think, more
useful: \emph{a screen's hits are substantially predictable from other screens' labels, largely through
recurrence and essentiality} --- which no within-experiment active learner or LLM agent can see --- but
this prior is not a discovery engine for genuinely new biology. We tested directly whether the
within-screen components add value \emph{over} the raw recurrence count: starting both from the same
single hit-frequency feature (5 seeds), adding online retraining and LLM verification moves the mean from
$0.243$ to $0.239$ (paired $-0.004$, screen-clustered $95\%$ CI $[\!-\!0.046, 0.028]$; higher on 6 of 9
screens but lower on average) --- \textbf{indistinguishable from, if anything slightly below}, the pure
recurrence ranking. So online adaptation and LLM verification do lift the \emph{feature-based} prior ---
against the matched final-configuration LOO, $0.231\!\rightarrow\!0.251$ ($+0.020$, CI $[0.007,0.035]$) ---
but we cannot claim they beat a pure recurrence ranking. All numbers here are produced by
\texttt{python -m waddington\_select.analysis.prior\_probes} and \texttt{.router\_analysis}.

\paragraph{Recurrent vs.\ novel hits (exploratory).} A sharper question than mean hit@R5: can any method
find hits that \emph{never} occur in the training screens --- biology recurrence cannot see? Splitting each
screen's hits into recurrent (a hit in $\ge\!1$ other screen) and novel (never), $65\%$ of all hits are
novel. A hit-frequency ranking found \textbf{none} of them in these runs (novel-recall $0.000$, as every
novel gene receives a zero recurrence score) while recovering $49.5\%$ of recurrent hits. The
\emph{feature-based} LOO prior does better on novelty (macro novel-recall $\mathbf{0.105}$, recurrent
$0.439$): its structural features flag a plausible hit even when the gene has never been a hit elsewhere.
The full Waddington does \emph{not} improve on this --- novel-recall $0.074$ (lower on 6 of 9 screens;
paired $-0.031$, screen-clustered $95\%$ CI $[\!-\!0.089, 0.007]$) while recurrent-recall rises to $0.462$
($+0.023$, $[\!-\!0.012, 0.054]$). The \emph{point estimates suggest} that the \emph{combined} within-screen
components (online retraining $+$ LLM endorsement) shift selection toward recurrent, high-confidence hits
and away from novel ones, \textbf{although neither paired interval excludes zero}; the comparison also does
not isolate whether the online ML or the LLM drives it. This pattern is \emph{consistent with, but does not
independently establish}, the interpretation of the LLM as a verifier of confident candidates rather than a
discoverer of new biology. We report it as exploratory (no tuning on it);
\texttt{python -m waddington\_select.analysis.novel\_hit\_analysis} and \texttt{.final\_system\_stats}.

\subsection*{Robustness to a leakage-free router}
\label{sec:honest-router}

The Method disclosed a real hazard: the shipped router reads the target's \emph{realized} hit rate to
pick the fusion weight (Method, ``What the router may and may not know''). We now test directly whether
that leakage carries the result. Under a protocol \textbf{frozen and committed before these numbers were
inspected} (\texttt{waddington\_select.router\_protocol}), we re-derive the fusion policy using only the
\emph{other} screens and metadata knowable \emph{before} the target screen runs (pool size, perturbation
modality, cell line) --- never the target hit rate. Two variants: \textbf{global-fixed}, one fusion
weight $w_{\mathrm{LLM}}$ chosen by leave-one-out over the other screens; and a \textbf{nested router},
$w_{\mathrm{LLM}}=f(n_{\mathrm{pool}})$ with thresholds tuned only on the other screens. Both use a
label-free metadata feature policy (DepMap dropped iff CRISPRa or curated-essentiality; cell-line-matched
DepMap dropped on its own cell line). We report leave-one-study-out at two strengths, kept distinct: one
removes the same-study group only from the router's \emph{config selection}; the \emph{full} version
additionally denies the cross-experiment \emph{prior} its siblings.

\paragraph{The stricter test: deny the prior its siblings.} The variants above route honestly but still
let the cross-experiment prior \emph{train on} same-study sibling screens. The strongest
leave-one-study-out also removes the sibling from the prior itself. This does bite: the mean falls from
$0.264$ to $\mathbf{0.239}$, almost entirely on two screens that lose an informative sibling ---
K562-Essential $0.587\!\rightarrow\!0.444$ (its Perturb-seq twin) and IL-2 $0.358\!\rightarrow\!0.308$
(IFN-$\gamma$). But this handicaps only the numerator. The identical removal drops the bare LOO prior in
lockstep ($0.243\!\rightarrow\!0.199$; the sibling-excluded prior of the ``what the prior learns''
analysis). Compared \emph{like-for-like} --- both the full system and the prior denied their siblings ---
the C-arm still beats the prior by $\mathbf{+0.041}$ (screen-clustered $95\%$ CI $[0.014, 0.078]$,
winning 7 of 9). So the same-study structure inflates the \emph{absolute} hit rates but not the
C-over-prior increment that is the actual claim: that increment comes from within-screen online
adaptation and LLM verification, which no sibling supplies.

\paragraph{Memory ($\Delta = +0.000$).}
Removing the cross-experiment memory prompt changes nothing: the paired average is exactly zero.
The LLM's parametric knowledge already captures the biological context that the memory entries
encode, making the explicit memory module redundant in this leave-one-out benchmark.
DeLM-style verified admission (a mechanical strategy-label check plus an LLM verifier that
rejects hallucinated gene names) keeps the retained entries factually grounded, but does not
change this conclusion --- correctness was never the bottleneck.

\paragraph{Online ML retraining ($\Delta = -0.012$).}
Freezing the ML model at the LOO prior produces the most consistent degradation of any ablation:
it hurts on 7 of 9 screens, with the largest losses on K562-GWPS ($-0.038$) and
K562-Essential ($-0.025$).
Online adaptation is the single most valuable component of the arm.

\paragraph{The ``LLM'' branch ($\Delta = -0.008$ on average, but bimodal) --- and an important caveat.}
C\,$-$\,LLM removes the entire LLM-branch endorsement term from the fusion. That term is \emph{not}
purely the LLM: it also carries the static-ML padding described in Table~\ref{tab:main}$^\dagger$.
The two screens with the largest effects are exactly the two with the most padding, so they must be
read carefully:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{K562-Essential ($-0.127$): this is \emph{not} evidence for LLM biology.} 86\% of the
  branch's genes there come from the static LOO ranker (the LLM names almost nothing that exists in
  the 623-gene pool). What the ablation removes is the \emph{static cross-experiment prior} being
  blended into the online model --- a real and valuable signal, but not LLM reasoning. We retract the
  earlier reading of this number.
  \item \textbf{Scharenberg22 ($+0.114$ when removed):} 68\% padding, so ``the LLM hurts here'' is
  likewise mostly ``the static prior hurts here'', conflicting with an unusually strong online ML
  signal (LOO AUC $=0.825$ on K562 DepMap features).
  \item \textbf{Steinhart ($-0.059$): this one does survive.} Padding is 43\%, so a majority of the
  branch is genuine LLM naming, and the gene-name ablation below independently confirms it.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Steinhart is also where one would \emph{predict} the LLM to matter most, for a reason visible in
Table~\ref{tab:datasets}: it is the gain-of-function (CRISPRa) screen, asking which genes make CAR-T
cells resist exhaustion \emph{when over-expressed}, whereas every DepMap feature we use is derived
from \emph{knockout} screens. A gene that is dispensable when deleted can still be potent when
over-expressed --- IRF4, one of Steinhart's strongest hits, is essential in only 6\% of DepMap cell
lines --- so the ML prior has little to say there, and the LLM's biological knowledge is what fills
the gap. Consistently, Steinhart has both the lowest hit rate of the nine screens (0.8\%) and the
largest gene-name-shuffle penalty ($-0.084$).

\paragraph{Gene-name semantics ($\Delta = -0.010$; $-0.084$ on Steinhart) --- the clean probe of LLM biology.}
This ablation is immune to the padding confound in a way C\,$-$\,LLM is not: the branch is still
present and still padded, but the LLM can no longer recognise the genes it is naming. Performance
collapses on Steinhart ($-0.084$) and drops on K562-Essential ($-0.051$), so the LLM's contribution
on pathway-specific tasks is genuinely mediated by \emph{biological gene-name knowledge} rather than
by the task description or by experimental feedback. Scharenberg22 again improves ($+0.078$).

\subsection*{Reasoning vs.\ recall: can structure replace the gene name?}

The shuffle ablation shows the LLM's contribution runs through gene-name knowledge, but it does not
show \emph{why} --- because it removes the name and supplies nothing in its place. The shuffled arm
must name identifiers out of an 18k space with no information whatsoever; it measures a blindfolded
guesser, not a reasoner. The question we actually care about is whether the LLM must \emph{recall}
biology from its weights, or whether it can \emph{reason} from structure we hand it.

We therefore ran a four-point decomposition. All four use the identical routing, fusion and update
harness; the \emph{only} thing that varies is what the LLM sees:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{A0} --- anonymized IDs, no candidate pool, no features (the shuffle arm above).
  \item \textbf{A0.5} --- anonymized IDs $+$ the ML-ranked candidate pool, \emph{no} features (control).
  \item \textbf{A1} --- anonymized IDs $+$ the pool $+$ each candidate's structural feature vector,
  plus, each round, the mean feature profile of the revealed hits vs.\ non-hits, so the LLM can induce
  a decision rule and apply it.
  \item \textbf{A2} --- real gene names (the C-arm).
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Two design points matter. A1 is never shown the ML confidence score: were it shown, ``reasoning''
would collapse into copying the GBM. And \textbf{A0.5 exists because A1 is handed an ML-ranked pool
that A0 is not} --- without that control, A1\,$-$\,A0 would confound ``the features'' with ``being
given a shortlist'', and on the two smallest screens (K562-Essential: 623 genes; Scharenberg22: 1029)
that shortlist is itself a strong prior.

\noindent
Three results, in order of how much they surprised us:

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{The shortlist is worth nothing} ($-0.005$, negative on 5 of 9). A1's gain is not an
  artefact of being handed ML-curated candidates.
  \item \textbf{The features are worth $+0.053$, and positive on all nine screens} ($+0.001$ to
  $+0.279$), every per-screen effect exceeding its seed s.d. --- the most consistent effect anywhere
  in this paper.
  \item \textbf{The gene name is worth $-0.012$.} With every gene renamed to a meaningless identifier
  and the structural features supplied, the system \emph{matches or slightly exceeds} the real-name
  arm (0.265 vs.\ 0.253). On average, \emph{the LLM does not need to know that a gene is IRF4; it needs
  the structure.}
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Is this the LLM \emph{reasoning}, or would any rule do?} A1 hands the LLM the feature vectors
and lets it induce a decision boundary. To test whether the \emph{LLM} is what matters, we replace it
with the most trivial boundary imaginable and change nothing else: score each candidate by its
projection onto the (hit-centroid $-$ non-hit-centroid) axis in the identical anonymized feature space,
take the top $B$, no language model (round~1 falls back to the static ranking, exactly as A1 pads). This
one-line rule scores \textbf{0.268} averaged over the nine screens --- it \emph{matches}, and marginally
exceeds, A1's 0.265, screen by screen (K562-Essential 0.587 vs.\ 0.597, Scharenberg22 0.571 vs.\ 0.584,
Steinhart 0.083 vs.\ 0.065, IL-2 0.351 vs.\ 0.343). So A1 is \textbf{not} evidence that the LLM reasons:
the value is that the structural features are linearly separable, and a centroid rule extracts it as
well as a language model does. What the decomposition establishes is therefore narrower --- and cleaner
--- than ``the LLM reasons'': it is that \emph{the gene name is dispensable once the structure is
supplied}, with the LLM merely one (here interchangeable, and strictly unnecessary) way to map that
structure to a choice.

\paragraph{Do these features smuggle in privileged information?} The structural features include the
anchor-relative ones the leakage audit flagged as privileged (51\% of anchors are themselves target
hits). If ``structure replaces names'' rode on that leak, it should collapse once the anchor features are
removed. It does not. Re-running the ladder in the anchor-free feature space, structure still gives
\emph{similar} performance to names --- A1 $0.241$ vs.\ A2 $0.249$ (paired $-0.007$, $95\%$ CI
$[\!-\!0.041, 0.029]$; no detected difference, though the interval does not establish equivalence) --- and
the linear rule does about as well as the LLM ($0.257$; $+0.008$ vs.\ names). Removing the privileged
features lowers both structure arms in step (they were the strongest single family), so the
\emph{contrast} is preserved: the gene name remains dispensable even when the structure the LLM sees
contains no target-hit leakage. (Reproduce with \texttt{WADDINGTON\_DROP\_ANCHOR\_FEATS=1}.)

\noindent
That average, however, hides a bimodality that is the whole point:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Steinhart: the name is worth $+0.080 \pm 0.013$}, the largest anywhere, and the
  features recover almost nothing ($+0.017 \pm 0.008$; A1 at 0.065 is even \emph{below} A0 at 0.075,
  and the two error bars do not come close to overlapping). This is exactly the screen of
  Table~\ref{tab:shap}: the one gain-of-function (CRISPRa) screen, where every DepMap feature is
  knockout-derived and does not transfer. The features we are able to hand the LLM are precisely the
  ones that are uninformative there, so nothing we supply substitutes for knowing what IRF4 does.
  \item \textbf{Scharenberg22: the name is worth $-0.135 \pm 0.011$} --- it actively hurts, consistent
  with the shuffle \emph{improving} that screen.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
So substituting structure for the gene name is achievable and essentially free on eight of nine
screens, and impossible on the one screen where biology is doing real work. We read this as a limit of
what our feature table \emph{contains}, not as proof that structure cannot suffice: our features are
anchor-relative PPI / co-expression / pathway overlap plus constraint and essentiality, and none of
them encodes the specific T-cell biology Steinhart rewards. The obvious reading of the bullet above ---
that Steinhart is special \emph{because} it is the gain-of-function screen --- is tempting, and wrong:
we tested it directly (next), and perturbation modality does not explain it.

One further calibration: \textbf{A0 (0.216) scores below the plain GBM (0.224)}. An LLM branch with
nothing to say does not merely fail to help --- at a 0.4 fusion weight it drags the hybrid below the
ML model it was meant to improve.

\subsection*{Testing the explanation: is it the gain-of-function modality? (No.)}

The story above rests on a sample of one: Steinhart is the only gain-of-function (CRISPRa) screen in
the benchmark, so ``gain-of-function makes the gene name matter'' is a hypothesis fitted to a single
point. We tested it. The same study that supplied our IL-2 and IFN-$\gamma$ screens (Schmidt et al.,
2022) ran \emph{both} a CRISPRi and a CRISPRa genome-wide screen for each cytokine, so the CRISPRa arm
is the matched gain-of-function counterpart of two screens we already have. We onboarded
\texttt{IL2\_crispra} and \texttt{IFNG\_crispra} from that arm, keeping everything fixed except the
modality: the same anchor-relative features (they are modality-agnostic --- activating vs.\ silencing
IL-2 biology shares the same anchors), the same hit convention, an activation-framed task prompt
(``genes that, when \emph{over-expressed via CRISPRa}, regulate IL-2 production''), and --- because
these large screens would otherwise route to the LLM-suppressed \texttt{ml\_heavy} regime --- the same
fusion weight as Steinhart ($w_{\mathrm{LLM}}=0.4$).

If gain-of-function were the cause, the gene name should be worth $\approx\!+0.08$ here too. It is not:

corrected-prompt run barely moved these numbers from a first run with a placeholder prompt, so this is
not an artefact of under-specifying the task; the name simply does not help here.

What \emph{does} distinguish Steinhart is the shape of its hit set: 145 concentrated hits (0.8\%)
dominated by textbook exhaustion regulators (IRF4, TOX, \ldots) that a language model knows cold,
against $\sim$900 diffuse top-movers ($\sim$5\%) on each CRISPRa cytokine screen, where recognizable
names are a small minority. Our loss-of-function-trained prior also transfers poorly to CRISPRa hits
(hit@R5 $\approx 0.12$ vs.\ $0.35$ on the CRISPRi twin), so the whole system is weak there. We leave the
hit-set-concentration hypothesis untested. The operational consequence is clear enough, though:
\textbf{we do not route by modality} --- the structure-only reasoning mode stays an opt-in choice, not
a default triggered by whether a screen is CRISPRa.

\subsection*{An accidental confirmation: the feature router already knew}

The C-arm selects its feature set per screen. That router was tuned \emph{empirically}, by trying
feature sets and keeping whatever scored best, long before we understood why. It converged on
excluding \textbf{all four DepMap features} for exactly two screens --- Steinhart and K562-Essential
--- because including them \emph{hurt}. The code comment recording this guessed at the reason
(``DepMap disrupts online learning for GD2 biology''), and that guess was wrong: Steinhart does not
measure GD2 biology at all (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}).

The real reason is the one above. \textbf{Every DepMap feature is derived from knockout screens},
and Steinhart is the one \emph{gain-of-function} (CRISPRa) screen: it asks which genes make CAR-T
cells resist exhaustion \emph{when over-expressed}. A gene that is dispensable when deleted can be
potent when over-expressed --- IRF4, among Steinhart's strongest hits, is essential in only 6\% of
DepMap cell lines --- so a knockout-derived prior is not merely uninformative there, it is actively
misleading, and the router learned to discard it.

\section*{Explainability: what is the LLM actually contributing?}

The ablations say \emph{how much} each component is worth; they cannot say \emph{where} the value
comes from. We therefore instrumented the arm to record, for every selected gene, a counterfactual
attribution against the ML model's own top-$k$:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{both} --- the LLM named it \emph{and} the ML would have selected it anyway;
  \item \textbf{LLM-only} --- the LLM named it and the ML would \emph{not} have selected it
        (this is the LLM's marginal contribution);
  \item \textbf{ML-only} --- the LLM did not name it; it entered on ML score alone.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
\textbf{Only genes the LLM actually named count as LLM picks.} The LLM branch pads its batch from the
static ranker (Table~\ref{tab:main}$^\dagger$), and crediting that padding to the LLM would make
``ML and LLM agree'' partly mean ``two ML models agree''. An earlier version of this analysis made
exactly that error: it reported an agreement bucket of $n=800$ at a $21.1\%$ hit rate, of which
$\approx$87\% was static-ranker padding. Excluding it sharpens the result considerably.

Pairing each pick with its revealed outcome gives the hit rate \emph{per source}
(9 screens $\times$ 5 rounds, $\approx$4{,}200 selected genes).
K562-GWPS is reported separately: it is the one screen routed through the two-stage policy
(the ML shortlists 384 candidates and the \emph{LLM} makes the final selection), so the
``ML-only'' category cannot exist there and pooling it would corrupt the rates.

\paragraph{The LLM is a verifier, not a proposer.}
The LLM is a \emph{poor proposer}: left to itself it picks genes that hit at $8.1\%$, well below the
ML's own $14.7\%$. But it is a \emph{strong verifier}: when it independently names a gene the ML also
ranks in its top-$k$, that gene hits at $46.5\%$ --- roughly three times the ML's rate. Such agreement
is rare (101 of $\approx$4{,}200 picks, i.e.\ $2.4\%$), which is precisely why the component is worth
so little on average and so much where it fires.
This single measurement explains the two results above that otherwise look unrelated:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item why \textbf{removing the LLM costs only $0.008$} --- the genes it proposes on its own are
        \emph{worse} than what the ML would have picked instead, so losing them costs nothing; what is
        lost is a rare but highly informative agreement signal;
  \item why \textbf{giving the LLM tools makes it worse} ($-0.047$, Table~\ref{tab:agent}) --- a free
        agent selects mostly on its own judgement, which is precisely the weakest of the three sources.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
The design implication is the opposite of the prevailing ``more agency'' direction: the LLM should
be used to \emph{re-weight} a calibrated model's candidates, not to choose freely.

\paragraph{Does endorsement add signal \emph{beyond} the ML score? (Yes --- but only where the ML is
already confident.)} The $46.5\%$ agreement rate has an obvious confound: genes both components endorse
might simply be the ones with the highest ML score, in which case the LLM adds nothing once that score
is held fixed. We test this directly. On the same eight weighted screens, we bin every selected gene
into ML-score deciles \emph{within its screen} (so each screen's model scale cancels) and, within each
bin, compare the hit rate of genes the LLM named against genes it did not. The value of naming is not
constant --- it is concentrated entirely in the top of the ML ranking:

\noindent
A logistic fit on the pooled genes puts a number on it: with the within-screen ML rank percentile and
screen fixed effects in the model, LLM endorsement carries an independent odds ratio of
$\mathbf{1.68}$ (screen-clustered bootstrap $95\%$ CI $[1.05, 2.52]$, $n=4{,}160$ genes over eight
screens). The interval excludes $1$, so endorsement predicts hits \emph{after} adjusting for the ML
score --- the agreement effect is not merely a restatement of ``high ML score.'' With only eight clusters
we also checked leave-one-screen-out: dropping each screen in turn keeps the adjusted OR between
$\mathbf{1.39}$ and $\mathbf{1.87}$ (all $>1$), and removing Steinhart --- the natural worry, being the
LLM's best screen --- leaves it at $1.65$, so no single screen drives it. But the tertile split
is the sharper statement: the LLM is a verifier of \emph{confident} ML candidates specifically, adding
nothing where the ML is unsure and confirming strongly where it is not. That is exactly the regime a
fixed-fusion policy exploits and a free agent discards.

\section*{What we claim, and what we do not}

Read plainly, the evidence supports a deliberately scoped set of claims:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Cross-screen recurrence and generic essentiality form a strong transferable prior.} A
  plain hit-frequency ranking over the other screens reaches $0.243$, matching the learned LOO-LightGBM;
  most of the transferable signal is genes that \emph{recur} as hits, not novel phenotype-specific biology.
  \item \textbf{Waddington improves the feature-based prior it actually deploys.} Against the
  \emph{matched} final-configuration prior --- the static LOO in the same no-anchor metadata feature space
  ($0.231$) --- the full system ($0.251$) is $+0.020$ (screen-clustered $95\%$ CI $[0.007,0.035]$, 7 of 9
  screens); against the paper's differently-configured LOO baseline ($0.217$) it is $+0.034$
  $[0.016,0.053]$. Both intervals exclude zero.
  \item \textbf{We do \emph{not} establish an advantage over a pure recurrence ranking.} Starting both from
  the same hit-frequency prior, adding online adaptation and LLM verification moves the mean only
  $0.243\!\rightarrow\!0.239$ (paired $-0.004$, CI including zero). On this nine-screen benchmark the
  within-screen components are not shown to beat recurrence.
  \item \textbf{The LLM's supported role is conditional verification, not discovery.} Endorsement predicts
  hits after adjusting for the ML score (adjusted OR $1.68$), but only among high-confidence ML candidates;
  it is not shown to surface biology beyond what the ML already ranks highly.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
In one sentence: \emph{cross-screen recurrence and essentiality provide most of the transferable signal;
Waddington integrates that prior into a constrained, conversational sequential system and reliably improves
it through within-screen adaptation and constrained LLM endorsement, but the available nine-screen
benchmark does not establish an advantage over a pure recurrence ranking.}

\section*{Limitations}

\paragraph{We cannot separate the LLM's biological knowledge from memorisation.}
This is the most important caveat on every LLM result above. Seven of the nine screens are published,
so their hit genes may sit in the model's training data, and a gene the model ``knows'' is a hit is
indistinguishable --- to our measurements --- from a gene it reasons its way to.

It is tempting to appeal to the Steinhart screen as a clean control: BioDiscoveryAgent introduced it
precisely because it was \emph{unpublished}, stating that it is therefore ``not part of the language
model's training data''. That argument was sound \emph{for their models} (Claude 3.5 Sonnet, whose
training cutoff precedes their release). \textbf{It does not transfer to ours.} BDA released the
screen in their public repository (\texttt{ground\_truth\_Steinhart\_crispra\_GD2\_D22.csv} and the
corresponding \texttt{topmovers} file) in 2024, whereas our LLM is Claude Haiku 4.5, released in late
2025. We therefore cannot claim the screen is outside our model's training data, and we withdraw the
otherwise attractive inference that ``the LLM contributes most on the one screen it provably cannot
have memorised''.

We judge the practical leakage risk to be low --- the hit set exists only as an 18{,}000-row table of
effect sizes and a binary \texttt{.npy} array, not the kind of artefact language models reliably
memorise --- but low is not zero, and the strong claim is not available to us.
Note also that the gene-name-shuffle ablation ($-0.084$ on Steinhart) does \emph{not} settle this:
shuffling gene names destroys memorised gene--hit associations and genuine biological knowledge
equally, so it cannot distinguish them.
Settling the question requires a screen released \emph{after} the model's training cutoff; none of our
nine qualifies.

\paragraph{Other limitations.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{The cross-experiment prior is largely recurrence, not discovery.} As the prior-probe
  analysis shows, its gain comes substantially from same-study sibling screens ($-0.066$ on paired
  screens when excluded) and from generic knockout essentiality (DepMap-only $\approx$ the full model,
  and a trivial cross-screen hit-frequency count matches it). It is near-random on screens whose hits
  are mostly novel. It predicts what recurs; it does not find new phenotype-specific biology.
  \item \textbf{Not a verbatim reproduction of BioDiscoveryAgent.} We use their data and non-essential
  convention. We \emph{did} run their agent as a controlled comparison (Table~\ref{tab:external}), but
  with our backend and model (Haiku 4.5) and small harness adaptations, not their exact published
  configuration; the controlled 0.105 is close to their reported 0.127 (Sonnet 3.5). Our headline gain
  over them is a cross-experiment prior they do not have, not a better agent.
  \item \textbf{The B baseline is padded}, on some screens heavily
  (86\% on K562-Essential); it is not a pure-LLM baseline (Table~\ref{tab:main}$^\dagger$).
  \item \textbf{Genuine ML/LLM agreement is rare} ($n=101$), so its hit rate carries a wide interval
  ($46.5\% \pm 9.7$). The \emph{ordering} of the three sources is robust; the exact rate is not.
  \item \textbf{The attribution excludes K562-GWPS}, whose two-stage route lets the LLM make the final
  selection, so an ``ML-only'' pick cannot exist there by construction.
  \item \textbf{Calibration is measured only over genes the agent chose to test}, so the score range is
  narrow by construction and the curve is flat in its interior.
  \item \textbf{Nine screens, five seeds, no prospective wet-lab validation.} The arm is stochastic,
  which is why every component claim is reported as a \emph{paired} delta.
\end{itemize}
